Portfolio Case Study

Customer Journey & Multi-Channel Attribution Strategy

Analyzing customer interactions across multiple marketing channels to understand how marketing contributes to conversions.

Independent Portfolio Case Study — Simulated Business Scenario

This project was created as part of my professional development to demonstrate customer journey analysis, attribution thinking, marketing measurement, and evidence-based decision-making. It does not represent work completed for a real client.

Business Situation

A growing e-commerce company was using multiple digital marketing channels to attract and convert customers. These channels included paid search, paid social, organic search, email marketing, and direct website traffic.

Leadership wanted to understand how customers were moving through the marketing funnel and which channels were contributing to conversions at different stages of the customer journey.

The primary concern was that relying on a single attribution model could lead to misleading conclusions about channel performance.

Business Challenge

The central question

How can the business evaluate the contribution of different marketing channels without assuming that the channel receiving the final click deserves all of the credit?

A customer may discover a brand through social media, return through organic search, interact with an email, and eventually convert after a branded paid-search interaction.

Treating only the final interaction as responsible for the conversion can hide important parts of the customer journey.

Objectives

  • Map the major stages of the customer journey.
  • Identify important customer touchpoints across channels.
  • Evaluate the limitations of single-touch attribution.
  • Compare alternative attribution approaches.
  • Develop a practical measurement framework.
  • Improve the quality of marketing investment decisions.

Customer Journey Framework

The customer journey was divided into several stages to distinguish between awareness, consideration, conversion, and retention.

1. Awareness

Customers first become aware of the brand or product through channels such as paid social, display advertising, organic content, or other discovery-based activities.

2. Consideration

Customers research the product, visit the website, compare options, read content, and potentially return through another marketing channel.

3. Conversion

The customer completes a desired action such as purchasing a product, submitting a lead, or completing another defined conversion event.

4. Retention

After conversion, email marketing, loyalty programs, remarketing, customer service, and personalized communication can influence repeat purchases and long-term customer value.

Channels Considered

  • Paid Search: captures customers actively searching for products or solutions.
  • Paid Social: supports discovery, awareness, engagement, and retargeting.
  • Organic Search: captures demand through unpaid search visibility and content.
  • Email Marketing: supports nurturing, retention, and repeat purchases.
  • Direct Traffic: represents users returning directly to the website.

Attribution Problem

A major measurement risk occurs when marketers interpret attribution as a simple question of which channel generated the conversion. In reality, multiple interactions can influence a customer's decision.

Example Customer Journey

  • Customer discovers the brand through paid social.
  • Customer later searches for the product organically.
  • Customer visits the website and reviews several products.
  • Customer receives a promotional email.
  • Customer returns through branded paid search.
  • Customer completes the purchase.

A last-click model would assign most or all conversion credit to the final interaction. However, that does not necessarily mean the earlier interactions had no influence.

Attribution Models Considered

Last-Click Attribution

Assigns conversion credit to the final measurable marketing interaction before conversion.

Strength: simple and easy to understand.

Limitation: can undervalue earlier interactions that contributed to awareness and consideration.

First-Click Attribution

Assigns credit to the first measurable marketing interaction.

Strength: useful for understanding acquisition and discovery.

Limitation: can undervalue later interactions that help move customers toward conversion.

Linear Attribution

Distributes conversion credit across multiple measured touchpoints.

Strength: recognizes that multiple interactions can contribute to a conversion.

Limitation: assumes interactions deserve relatively equal credit even when their influence may differ.

Data-Driven Attribution

Uses observed customer journey data to estimate how different interactions contribute to conversion outcomes.

Strength: can provide a more nuanced view of multi-touch customer journeys.

Limitation: requires reliable data, sufficient volume, appropriate measurement infrastructure, and careful interpretation.

Recommended Measurement Approach

Rather than relying on one attribution model as the sole source of truth, I would use a layered measurement approach.

  • Use last-click reporting for a simple operational view.
  • Use first-touch analysis to understand acquisition sources.
  • Use multi-touch analysis to investigate customer journeys.
  • Compare results across models rather than treating one model as absolute.
  • Use incrementality testing when practical to determine whether marketing activity actually causes additional conversions.

Key Metrics

  • Conversion rate
  • Customer acquisition cost (CAC)
  • Return on ad spend (ROAS)
  • Revenue by channel
  • Assisted conversions
  • Customer lifetime value (LTV)
  • Repeat purchase rate
  • Time to conversion
  • Number of marketing touchpoints before conversion

Risks & Limitations

Attribution analysis is only as reliable as the underlying measurement system.

  • Tracking gaps can create incomplete customer journeys.
  • Cookie and privacy limitations can reduce observable interactions.
  • Cross-device behavior may be difficult to connect accurately.
  • Direct traffic may contain users whose original acquisition source is unknown.
  • Correlation between a channel and conversion does not automatically prove causation.

Strategic Recommendation

The business should avoid making major budget decisions based solely on last-click attribution.

Instead, marketing leadership should evaluate channel performance using multiple perspectives, including customer journey behavior, conversion efficiency, profitability, retention, and controlled experimentation where possible.

Decision principle

Do not ask only, "Which channel received the conversion?" Ask, "What evidence suggests that this channel contributed to incremental business value?"

What I Would Do Next

  1. Audit tracking and conversion events.
  2. Document the major customer journey stages.
  3. Build consistent channel-level reporting.
  4. Compare first-touch, last-touch, and multi-touch perspectives.
  5. Identify major differences between attribution models.
  6. Investigate high-value customer segments separately.
  7. Design controlled tests where attribution uncertainty is high.
  8. Use the combined evidence to inform future budget allocation.

Key Takeaway

Effective attribution is not about finding one perfect model. It is about understanding the customer journey well enough to make better decisions with imperfect information.

A strong marketer recognizes that different channels can serve different roles throughout the journey and evaluates performance according to the business objective being measured.

PORTFOLIO CASE STUDY

CRM & Marketing Automation

Developing customer lifecycle, segmentation, retention, and marketing automation strategies to improve customer engagement and business performance.

Independent Portfolio Case Study — Simulated Business Scenario

This project was created as part of my professional development to demonstrate CRM strategy, customer lifecycle analysis, segmentation, marketing automation, retention thinking, and data-driven decision-making. It does not represent work completed for a real client.

Business Situation

A growing e-commerce company had successfully generated a large volume of website traffic and first-time purchases through paid search, paid social, organic search, and promotional campaigns.

However, the company was experiencing a common lifecycle marketing problem: acquiring customers was becoming more expensive while repeat purchase behavior remained inconsistent.

The business had customer information available through its website, email platform, and transaction system, but these data sources were not being fully translated into coordinated lifecycle marketing actions.

Leadership wanted to improve customer retention and increase the value generated from existing customers without relying entirely on additional acquisition spending.

Business Problem

The central problem was not simply a lack of customer data. The larger issue was that customer data was not being converted into a structured lifecycle strategy.

Customers at different stages of the relationship were receiving similar marketing messages even though their needs and behaviors were different.

For example, a first-time purchaser, an active repeat customer, and a customer who had not purchased for several months should not necessarily receive the same communication.

The strategic question was therefore:

How can customer data be used to create relevant lifecycle segments and automated marketing journeys that improve retention and customer value?

Customer Lifecycle Framework

I structured the customer lifecycle into several behavioral stages rather than treating the entire customer base as one audience.

1. New Prospect

A visitor who has demonstrated interest but has not yet completed a purchase. The primary objective is to reduce friction and move the prospect toward conversion.

2. First-Time Customer

A customer who has completed an initial purchase. The objective shifts from acquisition to building trust, encouraging product engagement, and creating an opportunity for a second purchase.

3. Repeat Customer

A customer who has purchased more than once. The focus becomes retention, personalized recommendations, and increasing customer lifetime value.

4. High-Value Customer

A customer demonstrating strong purchase frequency, order value, or long-term engagement. These customers may justify more personalized retention strategies and loyalty initiatives.

5. At-Risk Customer

A previously active customer whose engagement or purchase behavior has declined. The objective is to identify the decline early and test appropriate re-engagement strategies.

6. Dormant Customer

A customer who has not engaged or purchased within a defined period. The objective is to determine whether reactivation is economically justified.

Customer Segmentation Strategy

Rather than relying only on demographic information, I would prioritize behavioral segmentation because customer actions provide stronger signals for lifecycle marketing decisions.

  • Purchase frequency
  • Recency of last purchase
  • Average order value
  • Total customer revenue
  • Product category purchased
  • Email engagement
  • Website engagement
  • Previous promotional response
  • Time since last purchase
  • Customer lifecycle stage

This approach allows the business to move from broad customer lists toward behavior-based audiences.

Marketing Automation Strategy

The next step would be converting lifecycle insights into automated customer journeys.

Welcome Journey

New subscribers would enter a structured welcome sequence designed to introduce the brand, communicate value, reduce uncertainty, and guide the customer toward a first purchase.

Post-Purchase Journey

After purchase, customers would receive confirmation and educational communication before being introduced to relevant complementary products or future purchase opportunities.

Repeat Purchase Journey

Customers demonstrating repeat purchasing behavior would receive personalized recommendations based on previous interactions and product categories.

Abandoned Cart Journey

Customers who add products to their cart but do not complete checkout could enter a controlled recovery sequence designed to remind them of the product while avoiding excessive messaging.

Re-Engagement Journey

Customers showing declining engagement could enter a reactivation sequence. The messaging would become progressively more targeted rather than immediately relying on discounts.

High-Value Customer Journey

High-value customers could receive differentiated experiences designed to strengthen retention and increase long-term customer value.

Automation Logic

Effective automation should not simply send messages at predetermined intervals. It should respond to customer behavior.

For example, an automation system could use logic such as:

  • If a customer purchases, move them from the prospect journey into the post-purchase journey.
  • If the customer purchases again, move them into the repeat-customer segment.
  • If engagement declines for a defined period, evaluate the customer for an at-risk segment.
  • If the customer becomes highly valuable, apply a high-value customer segment.
  • If a customer re-engages, remove them from the reactivation journey.

This creates a dynamic lifecycle system rather than a collection of disconnected email campaigns.

Measurement Framework

The effectiveness of CRM and automation should be evaluated using business outcomes rather than email activity alone.

  • Repeat purchase rate
  • Customer retention rate
  • Customer lifetime value
  • Revenue per customer
  • Reactivation rate
  • Email conversion rate
  • Revenue generated by automated journeys
  • Unsubscribe rate
  • Customer engagement rate
  • Incremental revenue compared with a control group

A key principle is that automation performance should ultimately connect back to customer and business value.

Testing & Optimization

I would use controlled experimentation to determine which messages, timing strategies, offers, and customer segments produce incremental business value.

Potential tests could include:

  • Email subject line variations
  • Different post-purchase timing
  • Personalized versus generic recommendations
  • Discount versus non-discount reactivation messaging
  • Different customer segmentation thresholds
  • Different automation sequences

Where possible, a control group should be maintained so that the business can distinguish incremental impact from revenue that would have occurred naturally.

Strategic Recommendation

The recommended approach is to build CRM strategy around the customer lifecycle rather than treating automation as simply an email-scheduling tool.

The company should first establish reliable customer data and lifecycle definitions, then create behavioral segments, develop automated journeys, and measure their incremental business impact.

The highest priority should be retention opportunities where existing customers already demonstrate meaningful engagement or purchasing behavior.

This approach can improve marketing efficiency because the company is able to generate additional value from customers it has already acquired rather than depending entirely on continuously increasing acquisition spend.

Risks & Limitations

  • Customer data may be incomplete or inaccurate.
  • Automation can become excessive if communication frequency is not controlled.
  • Segmentation rules may become outdated as customer behavior changes.
  • Correlation does not necessarily prove that automation caused additional revenue.
  • Privacy, consent, and data governance requirements must be respected.
  • Discount-based retention strategies can reduce profitability if used without careful testing.

For these reasons, CRM automation should be continuously monitored and optimized rather than treated as a one-time implementation.

Business Impact

A well-designed CRM and marketing automation system can improve the relationship between acquisition, retention, and customer lifetime value.

Instead of viewing marketing as ending when a customer makes a purchase, the organization can use customer behavior to determine what relationship should happen next.

This creates a more connected marketing system in which customer data informs segmentation, segmentation informs automation, automation generates measurable interactions, and performance data informs future strategy.
